# 01 · What counts as cheating, by the numbers

*The Massive AI Manual — Student Papers guide*

The uncomfortable truth: nobody hands you a list of what's allowed. So here's
the list, built from how real teachers and honor codes actually talk.

---

## The one question that settles most cases

> **"When my teacher grades this paper, are they grading my understanding —
> or a machine's output?"**

If the paragraphs you submit contain understanding you couldn't reproduce without
the machine, you've crossed the line — even if the tool didn't write every word.

## The traffic-light table

Use this like a speed check. Green is almost always fine. Red is almost
always cheating. Yellow depends on your school (see
[guide 02](02-school-policy-translation.md)).

| 🚦 | Action | Plain-English verdict |
|----|--------|----------------------|
| 🟢 | Ask AI to explain a concept ("explain photosynthesis like I'm 12") | Learning. This is a tutor. |
| 🟢 | Ask AI for practice questions before the exam | Studying. |
| 🟢 | Ask AI to quiz *you* on your own draft | Study skills. |
| 🟢 | Use AI to generate a practice plan or study schedule | Planning, not producing. |
| 🟢 | Spell-check and grammar-check your *own* sentences | Editing your own words. |
| 🟢 | Brainstorm topic ideas, then pick and develop one yourself | Idea sparks. |
| 🟡 | AI generates an outline you then rewrite in your own structure | Some schools fine, some not. Disclose it. |
| 🟡 | AI critiques your draft and you fix the issues yourself | Feedback loop — usually fine if disclosed. |
| 🟡 | Paraphrasing tool at the sentence level | Gray zone; often flagged as contract-y. Learn to paraphrase instead. |
| 🔴 | AI writes the essay; you edit the wording | Ghostwriting. The ideas aren't yours. |
| 🔴 | Ask AI the exact assignment prompt and submit the answer | Submitting machine output as yours. |
| 🔴 | AI-generated citations you never opened | Fabrication, even if the essay is yours. |
| 🔴 | Translating a purchased or found essay to "make it yours" | Plagiarism, twice over. |
| 🔴 | Retyping AI output so it "reads like you" | Same thing, worse: deliberate deception. |

## By the numbers: where students actually get caught

Academic integrity offices don't spend their time sniffing for AI prose. They act
when something else goes wrong first. Approximate hierarchy of how AI misuse is
usually detected (from how honor-code cases are publicly described):

1. **The essay doesn't match the student.** In-class writing, discussion, or
   follow-up questions show the student can't explain their own paper.
2. **Fabricated sources.** A citation that doesn't exist is still the single
   easiest thing to catch. See [guide 05](05-verifying-citations.md).
3. **Wrong facts about the course.** AI doesn't know what happened in your class
   in week 3 — papers that "expand on the lecture" with invented lecture content
   stand out fast.
4. **Detector scores are a weak fourth place.** AI detectors are unreliable
   enough that many schools stopped treating them as proof. But a high score
   *plus* any sign above means a conversation you'd rather avoid.

## Three honest numbers to keep in your head

- **0** — the number of sources an AI has actually read, no matter how
  confident it sounds. (Read [guide 05](05-verifying-citations.md) before you
  trust any citation.)
- **1** — one disclosure sentence keeps you honest even in the gray zone.
  (Read [guide 06](06-disclosure-statements.md).)
- **∞** — how many free uses of "explain this to me like a tutor" you get
  without consequence. Asking AI to *teach* is the highest-value, zero-risk
  use in this whole manual.

## Scenario drill

Test yourself against the one question. Answers below the fold.

1. You paste your own history draft into AI and ask "which paragraph is weakest,
   and why?" You fix it yourself.
2. You ask AI to "write a strong thesis about the New Deal," copy the thesis
   verbatim, and build the essay around it.
3. You ask AI to quiz you with flashcards on the Spanish Inquisition.
4. AI writes your conclusion because "I ran out of ideas there."

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Honest.** AI is critiquing; you're doing the understanding.
2. **Cheating at most schools.** The thesis *is* your argument. Have AI ask you
   questions until your own thesis emerges ([guide 04](04-outlining-and-feedback-loops.md)).
3. **Honest.** Tutoring, textbook.
4. **Cheating at most schools.** "Ran out of ideas" is exactly the moment AI
   should ask *you* questions, not write for you ([guide 03](03-ai-as-tutor-not-ghostwriter.md)).
</details>
