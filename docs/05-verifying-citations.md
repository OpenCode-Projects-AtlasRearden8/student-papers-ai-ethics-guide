# 05 · Verifying citations

*The Massive AI Manual — Student Papers guide*

Fabricated citations are the single most common way AI-assisted papers are
caught. AI doesn't have a library — it has a *memory of how sources look*.
Sometimes it remembers real ones. Sometimes it stitches together plausible
fakes: real authors, real journals, titles that sound perfect and exist nowhere.

This rule is non-negotiable:

> **A citation generated or suggested by AI does not exist until you have
> opened it — or held the book — with your own eyes.**

---

## Where the hallucination comes from

Modern AI chatbots predict likely text, not verified facts. When you ask for
"sources about X," the model produces the *shape* of a source — author names in
the right style, a journal name from the right field, a year that sounds
reasonable. The result is statistically plausible and factually unverified.

Cheap tells that a citation may be invented:

- "According to a 2023 study published in the American Journal of Educational
  Psychology..." — that title may not exist
- Author + specific year + confident claim, with no URL or DOI to check
- "Smith et al. (2019) found that 78% of students..." — the precise-sounding
  statistic is a classic fabrication artifact
- A perfect source from an obscure niche journal, exactly on your narrow
  topic — more likely fictional than a well-known book you'd recognize

## The 5-step verification pass

Given to you as a student workflow. It takes ~2 min per citation — cheap
insurance against a misconduct case.

1. **Search the exact title in Google Scholar.** Any quote variation must work.
   Copy/paste the *exact* title from your bibliography, not your memory.
2. **Search `"author last name" "short title phrase"`.** Fuzzy matches: pull up
   the authors' publication lists if you find nothing.
3. **Open the source.** Just opening the abstract or first page — it must
   support the *claim you attached to it*.
4. **Read enough to support your claim.** You're citing a central finding, not
   a vibe. Cite what the article *actually says* about your exact point. An AI
   summary of the abstract does not count as reading it (see
   [guide 03](03-ai-as-tutor-not-ghostwriter.md)).
5. **Record the real citation from the source's own page.** Citation details
   (year, volume, pages) come from the real metadata — not from the AI, not
   from your memory.

**Anyone of these fails → the citation is dead. Rewrite the claim or find a
real source.** A dead citation near a statement in a paragraph you wrote is
still, coldly, your fabrication ([guide 01](01-what-counts-as-cheating.md)).

## The claims test (the part most students miss)

A source must support the **claim** you've appended it to, not merely exist.

> Wrong: *Students benefit from structured reading practice.* → cited to a
> paper about "reluctance to read among 2nd-graders in rural districts."
> Related topic, wrong claim. The paper says nothing about your sentence.

A 30-second prep assist from AI (as a tutor, never as a fact source —
see [guide 03](03-ai-as-tutor-not-ghostwriter.md)):

> "Here's my bibliography: [PASTE], plus one sentence from my paper next to
> each source. Sort my claims into 'this source could plausibly support it,'
> 'it can't,' and 'I need a different source for this.'"

This is tutoring you to catch your own error — not outsourcing the fact-check.
**You still read each source yourself before citing it.**

## Red flags on an AI-supplied bibliography

| Flag | Why it's suspicious |
|------|---------------------|
| Two sources from the same obscure journal, same year | Fabrications tend to cluster |
| A very famous person writing outside their field ("Einstein, A. (2019). Neural networks in college grading") | Dead giveaway |
| Year, volume, and page numbers that don't match the journal's real records | Copy-paste realism gone wrong |
| A swollen bibliography where your essay actually cites only 3 of 8 sources | Padding that reviewers check |
| "Per the sources," when you haven't read a single one | Academic hazmat |
| A bibliography you never typed — the chat window built it | Fabrication straight from the source |

## Prefer real sources in the first place

Most schools give you library databases (JSTOR, EBSCO, ProQuest, PubMed).
Those contain reviewed, real literature — start there every time. AI is
excellent *after* you have sources: ask it to explain what you read, quiz you
on it, and show where your argument is thin (see
[guide 04](04-outlining-and-feedback-loops.md)). The proven order:

1. **Find real sources** — library database, Google Scholar, the course readings.
2. **Read them yourself** — or at minimum the abstract and the sections you cite.
3. **Then use AI to explain, question, and critique** — tutor mode, no writing.

## Quick self-check

Tick these before submitting a bibliography:

- [ ] Opened every single source myself?
- [ ] Each source supports the exact claim it's attached to?
- [ ] Citation details copied from the source's own metadata?
- [ ] Zero sources remaining in the file that only exist in a chat window?
- [ ] Comfortable reading any one of these sources aloud in class?

If you can honestly tick all five — that bibliography is *yours*.
