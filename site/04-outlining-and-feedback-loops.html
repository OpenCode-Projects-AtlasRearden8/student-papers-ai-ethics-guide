Essays and Papers with AI, Honestly

The Massive AI Manual — Student Papers guide

Two moments in essay-writing tempt students toward the ghostwriter: the blank page at the start (outlining) and the deadline hour at the end (revision). Both become safe — and better — when you flip who does the thinking.

The blank-page problem, flipped

Weak: "Write me an outline for an essay on social media and mental health."

You get generic filler the teacher has seen four hundred times. And every sentence after it sounds generic, because you're filling in someone else's skeleton instead of your own.

Strong: interrogate the AI until the outline falls out of your head.

The Five Prompt Outline Method (~15 minutes)

Run these in order. Write your own answers down — pen and paper is perfect. The AI never outlines for you; it interrogates you.

  1. "What could I argue in an essay about [TOPIC]? For each idea, ask me a question that would reveal whether I actually believe it." — You pick a thought you can defend.
  1. "I want to argue [YOUR THESIS]. Give me the three strongest objections a smart skeptic would raise. Don't give me rebuttals — I'll write those." — Now the essay has a spine: claim, counter, response.
  1. "What would I need to research to answer objection #1? List the questions I'll need sources to answer." — This becomes your actual research list. (Real sources: guide 05.)
  1. "Here's my outline, which I wrote myself: [PASTE]. Attack it the way a grading committee would: holes, order problems, weak links between sections. Then ask me how I'll fix each problem." — Fixes stay yours.
  1. "Based on my answers above, grade my outline's readiness to write out of 10 and tell me why it's not a 10." — Green light is a 7+ with reasons in your words.

The honesty check for outlines

If, step 5, your outline contains arguments you couldn't defend out loud, redo step 2. An outline of borrowed arguments becomes a paper of borrowed paragraphs (guide 01 explains how that ends).

The revision loop, flipped

Feedback (critique → your fix) is honest. Rewriting (AI edits your text) is the drift zone. Two versions:

The human-hands version (recommended, disclose as feedback)

You:   "Here's my draft: [PASTE]. Acting as a demanding professor, list the
        five weakest things — argument, evidence, structure, clarity — and
        say WHY each is weak. Do not rewrite anything."

AI:    (critique)

You:   Now fix each weakness yourself, in your own hands, in your draft.
       This is the whole deal: critique from AI, editing from you.

You:   "I fixed #1-#5. Now grade my argument like a strict grader and tell
        me what's still weak."

Loop this 2–3 times. That's a genuine feedback loop, and mysterious AI-edited prose never touches the file.

The hands-on version (only where your school allows it)

If — and only if — your personal policy page (guide 02) allows tool-assisted editing:

  1. Feed the AI your draft. It may suggest rewording.
  2. You accept or reject every line in a tracked-changes view — never "fix all."
  3. Reread the final version out loud. If you trip on words, reword them in your own voice until you'd say them.
  4. Disclose it (guide 06). Hands on keyboard or not, AI made the text better, and that fact belongs in your disclosure.

The two questions that decide which loop you're in

Ask after every AI-assisted round on a draft:

  1. "Do I want to fix it, because I understand it, or fix it because the bot said so?" If you can't explain the fix, it's not yours.
  2. "If the deadline was extended by a week, would I keep these edits?" If you'd actually rewrite the section yourself, you're editing. If you'd just polish what's there, you rode the bot.

A revision-tracking template

Keep each loop's date, what the AI was asked, what you changed:

DateWhat I asked AI to critiqueWhat I changed myself
Oct 14Thesis clarity of introRewrote the thesis in my own words
Oct 21Evidence gaps ¶ 4Added study from-across-sources I found myself

Sentences I changed may be graded as mine. Sentences the AI rewrote may not be. When in doubt — disclose (guide 06).

What the finished loop looks like

  • You can restate your thesis out loud without peeking.
  • Every claim traces to a source you touched or to your own reasoning.
  • Weaknesses were fixed by you, not pasted in by one click.
  • You can reread the whole paper and see your fingerprint in every section — zero paragraphs that sound like a stranger wrote them.

Now paper two takes less time, because you learned — not because the tool learned for you. That's the entire point of school, honestly.
