The Massive AI Manual — Student Papers guide

Three ready-to-adapt examples of an AI-era honor code: a full policy for a school or department, a one-page syllabus insert for a single teacher, and a personal honor pledge for a student. Adapt before adopting — even the best template must match your institution's actual rules (guide 02).

Example A — Full school/department policy (~1 page)

AI Use Policy (Draft for adaptation)

1. Purpose. AI tools may help students learn; they may not replace the learning this course exists to produce. All submitted academic work must represent the student's own understanding, produced under the conditions stated below.

2. Default rule. Unless an assignment states otherwise, AI tools may be used in tutoring roles only: explaining concepts, generating practice questions, and critiquing a student's own draft at the student's request. Students must be able to explain any work they submit.

3. Permitted without disclosure. Tutoring as defined in §2 asked for by the student; AI-generated practice materials the student critiques; use of AI for tasks that are not submitted for credit (emails, schedules).

4. Permitted with disclosure. Idea brainstorming; outline development where the student writes the outline; AI critique the student acts on individually. Required disclosure format is the AI Use Statement (see guide 06), placed at the end of the submitted document.

5. Prohibited.

  • Submitting AI-generated prose, in whole or in substantial part, as the student's own writing.
  • Submitting AI-suggested citations that have not been verified against the actual sources.
  • Circumventing tool-permission rules, for example by having another person or account run AI work on your behalf.
  • Fabricating an interview, observation, dataset, or source.

6. Verification. Where suitability is in doubt, the course instructor may (a) ask the student questions about their submitted work, (b) require in-class writing, or (c) review a student's working notes and drafts. Adaptations of this policy may not be less protective of the learner or more lax than the school's underlying honor code.

7. Consequences. Violations are handled under the school's existing academic integrity process. Traditional definitions of cheating and plagiarism apply fully to AI-era artifacts.

Example B — One-page syllabus insert (for a single course or class)

This class's AI rules (plain-English version)

You may always: use AI like a tutor — ask it to explain, to quiz you, to brainstorm with you, to quiz you on your own draft. Typing any of what it says into a submitted assignment is a violation.

You may, with a one-sentence AI Use Statement at the end of your paper: brainstorm argument ideas with AI; get AI feedback on your own outline; get AI critique of your own draft — and then revise yourself.

You may never: paste my assignment prompt into an AI and submit what comes back; keep AI content in your text; cite a source you found via AI without actually reading it; submit any thinking you outsource to a machine as if it were yours.

How I check: I will ask questions about your paper in class. You should be able to defend every paragraph. Uncertainty on your part is a normal part of learning — I grade growth, not perfection. I cannot, and will not, treat AI-detector scores as proof. I will judge the paper and your ability to discuss it.

How to stay clearly inside the rules: include the AI Use Statement whenever you've used an AI tool in any way. If in doubt, disclose more, not less.

Example C — Personal honor pledge (for a student)

My AI Honor Pledge

I take a certain amount of AI help with my schoolwork. This is a line I won't cross, and I'm writing it down ahead of time because it's harder to see under deadline pressure.

What AI may do for me: explain things I don't understand; ask me questions about my own work; generate practice questions and materials I critique; critique my drafts so I can revise.

What AI may never do: write sentences that end up in my submitted work; choose my thesis or my argument; make paragraph-sequencing decisions in a way I can't explain afterward; hand me a citation I haven't personally opened.

My test: if the deadline was pushed a week, would I still keep my paper as it is, or would I want to rewrite the parts the bot touched? If I can't redo those ideas from my own head, the AI wrote them, not me.

My reminders: disclose generously (guide 06); verify every AI-suggested citation (guide 05); redo this pledge each term, because pressure changes for each class.

Signed: ____________________ Date: __________

Adaptation checklist (for whoever adopts)

Before using any of these, change:

  • ☐ Term definitions to match your institution's academic integrity policy.
  • ☐ §2 default rule to reflect what your teachers actually permit —

this stems from your school's genuine rules, not from our manual.

  • ☐ Disclosure format to the one your school actually uses.
  • ☐ Consequences to point to your real process (honor council, academic

integrity office, etc.).

  • ☐ Grade level: simplify vocabulary for younger students; the traffic-light

language of guide 01 works well for middle school.

For teachers: how to detect honestly

You don't need unreliable AI detectors. Use these proven methods instead:

  1. Talking about the work. Ask a student to explain their own argument for two minutes. Ghostwritten papers collapse there.
  2. In-class timed writing on the same topic at least once per term.
  3. Draft trails. Require an outline and a rough draft — ghostwritten work often appears fully formed.
  4. Read the AI's version. Reconstruct the student's likely prompt and see how close the essay is to the machine's typical output — structure and vocabulary mimicry is usually visible once you look.
  5. Citation audit — guide 05 shows how fast fabricated sources reveal themselves.

Detectors score high on false positives against non-native English speakers and against students with plain, clear writing styles; treat any detector score as a conversation starter with the student, never as a verdict.
